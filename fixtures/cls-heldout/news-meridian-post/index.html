The night trains are coming back, one sleeper at a time

After two decades of cuts, operators are ordering new carriages. The harder problem is finding a path through the timetable at three in the morning.

Bastien OkaforEurope transport correspondent · 14 min read

The carriage smells of new carpet and cold coffee. It left the factory eleven days ago and has since crossed four borders, changed locomotives five times and waited, on one occasion, for ninety minutes in a siding outside Linz while a freight train carrying cars went by.

That wait is the story of the night train in miniature. The rolling stock exists again; what does not exist is room. Europe's main lines are busiest with freight exactly when sleepers need them, and maintenance crews own the hours in between.

Operators have ordered more than three hundred sleeping cars since 2021, the largest such order in a generation. Most will not carry a passenger before next year, and several routes announced with fanfare have quietly slipped a timetable or two.

“We can build a bed in six months. A path through Germany at night takes three years.”

The economics are unforgiving. A sleeper carries perhaps a third of the passengers of a day coach and makes one journey in the time a regional unit makes six. What it sells instead is a hotel night that happens to end five hundred kilometres away.

The night network in numbers

  • 31 regular overnight routes in 2019, 48 planned for 2027
  • Average occupancy on the Vienna services: 82%
  • Typical access charge per train-kilometre at night: €4.10

Whether that is enough depends on who is asked. Ministers talk about modal shift. The people who draw timetables talk about a single track through the Brenner and a tunnel that will not open until the next decade.
